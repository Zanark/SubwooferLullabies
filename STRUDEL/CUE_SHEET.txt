WHAT CHANGED FOR CALM

  - 60 BPM instead of 92. This is a compositional choice, not a magic heart-rate
    target. Simply stretching the old 60-bar form would also exceed three minutes.
  - 66 piano notes instead of 295; approximately 0.41 attacks/second instead of
    1.88 across the active score. Notes are at least a second long, with long rests.
    Piano range is now G3-A4, rather than D4-F6.
  - 20 chord attacks and 20 bass roots over 160 active seconds, normally one per
    eight seconds. No walking bass, offbeat chord repetitions, chromatic bass runs,
    swing, brushed beat, drum build or fast ornaments.
  - Open minor/major ninths, sixths and suspended voicings retain jazz color.
    Altered dominant/b9 tension is removed. The music develops through small melody
    and harmony changes, not louder or busier sections.
  - Piano attack is softened to 50ms; electric-piano chords bloom over 140ms.
    Instrument gains are lower, without treating quietness alone as relaxation.
    Extra keyboard delays are removed so echoes do not refill all the new space.
  - The sparse source arrangement has 121 attack clusters instead of 685, counting
    simultaneous chord tones as one attack per instrument. This is about an 83%
    reduction in attacks per second. It is a score measurement, not a stress score.

Underwater treatment remains throughout, including the ending. Piano cutoff varies
slowly per note between 850-1150 Hz, electric piano is filtered at 850 Hz, and bass
at 580 Hz. Narrow stereo placement and separate soft reverbs provide distance.
A quieter, non-pulsing 120-300 Hz brown-noise wash adds water-like softness. These
are sound-design choices, not a physical underwater simulation or breathing guide.

The developer lowered water gain to 0.1 after the first jazz pass. This rewrite
preserves that CURRENT gain, the exact envelope and the loved delay/reverb settings:
delay 0.45, delaytime 45/92 seconds, feedback 0.5, room 0.6, roomsize 2, roomfade 0.1.
There are now 14 isolated droplets instead of 69. Pitch placement/timing is newly
written, but the droplet treatment stays intact on orbit 3; no band filter affects it.
The 45/92 delay time intentionally remains in seconds rather than slowing with BPM.
The low wash releases over four seconds after cycle 40. Export all the way to 44.

RESEARCH BASIS FOR THE DEEP-CALM REWRITE

The evidence supports broad low-arousal design directions, not a guaranteed result
for this listener or a uniquely calming tempo. The user's report that the previous
arrangement felt fast is the decisive local feedback. Sixty BPM, eight-second chord
changes and the new note counts are composer-chosen targets, not clinical thresholds.

1. Eerola, Friberg & Bresin (2013), "Emotional expression in music: contribution,
   linearity, and additivity of primary musical cues."
   https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/full
   Full text reviewed. In a controlled synthesized-excerpt experiment, lower musical
   speed, softer dynamics, darker timbre and more legato articulation supported
   perceived peacefulness. The speed cue was non-simultaneous onsets per second,
   not simply metronome BPM. This motivates thinning the score as well as slowing it;
   it does not prove that density independently matters more than beat tempo.
   Higher register also supported peacefulness in that experiment. Our lower piano
   register is an arrangement choice, not a claim that low notes are always calmer;
   the isolated high droplets retain their sparkle.

2. Bernardi, Porta & Sleight (2006), "Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and respiratory
   changes induced by different types of music in musicians and non-musicians:
   the importance of silence."
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1860846/
   Full text reviewed. Faster selections accompanied greater activation in a small
   study; a two-minute silent pause produced particularly clear reductions.
   This supports taking space seriously, but two-second musical rests are NOT the
   studied two-minute pause. No reliable one-to-one heartbeat/breath entrainment or
   ideal BPM is established. Our rests are compositional breathing room only.

3. Gomez & Danuser (2007), "Relationships between musical structure and
   psychophysiological measures of emotion."
   https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.7.2.377
   https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17516815
   Indexed abstract reviewed; full text not verified. Faster, more accented and
   staccato excerpts were associated with greater activation. This supports removing
   driving rhythmic layers and strong accents; it does not make brushes inherently
   agitating or establish a particular synthesizer attack time as therapeutic.

4. Witek et al. (2014), "Syncopation, Body-Movement and Pleasure in Groove Music."
   https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0094446
   Full text reviewed. Moderate syncopation in funk drum patterns produced strong
   pleasure and desire to move. Groove can be enjoyable without serving this request
   for stillness. Removing continuous swing/brush subdivisions is a brief-specific
   decision; the paper does not establish a relaxation-specific swing ratio.

5. Lahdelma & Eerola (2016), "Mild Dissonance Preferred Over Consonance in Single
   Chord Perception."
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4934671/
   Full text reviewed. Major ninth, minor ninth and minor seventh chords were highly
   preferred, while the major triad had the lowest tension. Preference is not the
   same outcome as relaxation. T02 keeps gentle jazz extensions but avoids altered
   dominant stabs, dense low-register clusters and frequent harmonic surprises.

6. Cheung et al. (2019), "Uncertainty and Surprise Jointly Predict Musical Pleasure
   and Amygdala, Hippocampus, and Auditory Cortex Activity."
   https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067
   https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31708393
   Abstract reviewed; full text inaccessible during research. Pleasure depended on
   the interaction of uncertainty and surprise, not a rule that maximum repetition
   or novelty is best. Our through-composed sections retain recognizable contours
   and tonal centers, developing without increasing density. The study was not a
   relaxation or underwater-music trial.

7. Adiasto et al. (2022), "Music listening and stress recovery in healthy individuals:
   A systematic review with meta-analysis of experimental studies."
   https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0270031
   https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9205498/fullTextXML
   Full-text XML reviewed. The pooled effect across 14 studies was not statistically
   significant and was heterogeneous. This is not proof that music cannot help;
   it cautions against guaranteed stress reduction or a universal tempo prescription.

SYNTHESIS AND LIMITS

The rewrite combines less musical activity, longer spaces, soft entrances, restrained
accents and stable jazz colors. Bass and chord entrances coincide rather than
alternating to create a faster hidden pulse. Only the loved water keeps audible
feedback echoes; fewer fresh drops give those echoes room.

Exact EQ cutoffs, reverb sizes, envelope milliseconds and underwater noise settings
are sound-design judgments, not findings from the papers above. No claim is made
for 432-Hz tuning, breathing control, sleep induction or a universally "most relaxing"
song. The developer's audition remains the arbiter of whether this version feels
calming, unrushed and underwater; source-event counts cannot measure that experience.

READING THE CODE

setcpm(BPM / 4) sets the clock; this collection treats a cycle as a four-beat bar.
stack(...) mixes parallel layers, equivalent in purpose to multiple "$:" lines.
note(...) describes pitches; sound(...) chooses synthesized timbre or noise.
Spaces sequence notes; commas inside brackets make chords; "~" means a rest.
<...> advances one element each bar; [ ... ] subdivides that element.
arrange([8, phrase], ...) places musical sections; arrange itself repeats.
filterWhen(t => t >= 0 && t < N) makes the other cues finite on a fresh clock.
The opening's at(seconds, span, phrase) helper places phrases against film timing.
clip and the ADSR controls shape note length; gain balances the instruments.
orbit isolates shared reverb/delay. Keep Multi Channel Orbits off for stereo WAVs.
